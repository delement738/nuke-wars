# Nuke Wars V1.1 — Design Consultation: The Second-Order War

> **PROPOSAL — this is not the spec.** The authoritative rules remain
> `nuke-wars-v1-spec.md`, and nothing below is implemented. Each step becomes real
> only when it is built in its own session with the spec amended as part of that
> change (CLAUDE.md workflow). Like `v2-backlog.md`, never implement from this file
> directly — implement from a session goal that cites it.
>
> **Direction decided with the designer, 2026-08-30:** (1) combat stays fully
> deterministic — no dice; (2) changes land as incremental V1.1 steps, each with a
> before/after `npm run soak`.
>
> **Amended the same day, on two designer additions** (see §5 Steps 1–2): battle-report
> notifications and a single, publicly-located interceptor base. Both are smaller than
> missile flight time and both attack numbers §1 already indicts, so they move to the
> front: **build order is now battle reports → single public base → flight time.** The
> flight-time brief, with TypeScript types shown for approval per the workflow rule,
> stays `docs/next-session-flight-time.md`; the old Step 3 (reveal-to-defend
> interception) is superseded by the new Step 2 and marked as such below.

The complaint this answers: *the game rewards one simple strategy — whoever advances
and covers more ground with drones and launchers wins — and it does not reward
second- and third-order thinking, long-term strategy, or reading the opponent.*

---

## 1. The complaint, measured

`SOAK_MATCHES=60 SOAK_SEED=3 npm run soak` (2026-08-30, current `main`). The harness
agrees with the complaint, and sharpens it: mostly, *nobody* wins.

| Measurement | easy mirror | medium mirror | hard mirror |
|---|---|---|---|
| Armistice (timeout draw) | **59/60** | 28/60 | 20/60 |
| Mean match length (cap 25) | 25.0 | 15.2 | 13.0 |
| Missiles fired, per side per match | 10.9 | 3.4 | **3.6** |
| ... intercepted | 0.0 | 0.4 | **0.3** |
| Interceptor bases killed, per side | 0.16 | 0.01 | **0.01** |
| Sides that ever found a site | 10/120 | 58/120 | **58/120** |
| Enemy home zone photographed | 4.6% | 26.3% | **25.7%** |

Head-to-head: **hard beats medium 38–30 with 52 draws** (120 matches, both seats).

Four readings of those numbers, each a design verdict:

1. **The offensive system is idle 91% of the time.** 3 launchers × 13 rounds = 39
   launcher-rounds at hard; 3.6 are spent firing. The game's only offensive verb is
   almost never worth saying.
2. **The defensive system runs about once every three matches per base.** 0.3
   intercepts and 0.01 base kills per side. Interceptor bases — half the placement
   puzzle, the whole §10 geometry system — are effectively scenery.
3. **Half of all sides never find a single enemy site.** The bunker hunt is the
   game's stated heart (§1), and 52% of players never get a heartbeat.
4. **A full tier of skill converts to +8 net wins in 120 games.** If being much
   better barely wins more, the strategy space is flat — which is the complaint,
   proved.

**And the search geometry is unwinnable by construction.** A home zone is 6 rows ×
16 cols = 96 hexes. One clean drone flight reveals a 3-wide corridor ~7 hexes long ≈
21 new hexes; a perfect non-overlapping sweep needs ~5 flights plus transit, and the
drone dies 2.3 times a match at 2 blind rounds each. Best observed coverage: ~26%.
**The search space is larger than the match.** The hunt is not a hunt — it is a
lottery on whether your lane crossed their hex, and that is exactly why "sweep more,
advance more" is the only strategy: it buys more tickets.

## 2. Five structural causes

1. **Advance is free, so advance is not a decision.** Moving forward costs nothing,
   risks nothing detectable, and strictly widens what you threaten. When one option
   strictly dominates, it is an instruction, not a choice.
2. **Recon has no opportunity cost — even dying is profitable.** Unlimited respawns,
   and a shot-down drone hands its owner a 7-candidate base clue (§11). The correct
   drone order is the same every round, so the headline system poses no question.
3. **The defender makes one decision, at setup, then spectates.** There is no
   defensive order in the game. Interception is automatic, bases never act, the
   bunker sits. Half of a duel is missing.
4. **Information is monotonic, and the game contains exactly one bit of deception.**
   Nothing a player does can make the enemy's map *wrong* — static reveals are
   permanent and always true, contacts are always true. The decoy is a single
   binary bluff, usually spent by round 8; the other 17 rounds contain zero
   deception. **You cannot out-think an opponent whose beliefs you have no lever
   on.** This is the root cause of "no second- or third-order thinking."
5. **Every round is identical, so the game has no arc.** Round 20 plays exactly
   like round 3; the 25-round cap is doing the job that escalation design should.

## 3. The shape the game wants

Three acts, where **each act makes the previous act's information obsolete** — that
is what keeps a match alive instead of decided-but-not-over.

- **Act I — The Screen (rounds ~1–5).** No contact is geometrically possible yet
  (§7's tuning note). Today this is dead air; it should be doctrine, opening lane
  choice, and first feints — the question being *how much of my plan does my
  opening reveal?*
- **Act II — The Hunt (~6–15).** Today the whole game, and a lottery. Should be a
  duel of probes and reveals in which the attacker **spends missiles to force the
  defender to answer questions** (Steps 1, 3, 5).
- **Act III — The Kill or the Escape (15+).** Today the clock just runs out. Should
  be escalation pressure, convoy chases, the decoy test, and real dead-hand fear
  (Steps 2, 4, 6).

## 4. Randomness — ruled out (decided 2026-08-30)

Design pillar 2 stands: no dice in combat, `resolve()` still reads no RNG. Recorded
so no future session reopens it casually:

1. **Dice destroy the read.** In a deterministic game a loss proves you were
   out-thought, which is what makes a player model the opponent next match. Noise
   gives the loss somewhere else to live, and the modelling — the thing this whole
   consultation is trying to create — stops.
2. **The game already has a randomiser: the opponent.** Simultaneous hidden orders
   produce all the subjective uncertainty needed. Dice double the noise while
   halving the information content of every outcome.
3. **Third-order thinking needs stable inference.** "They think I think they're at
   X" is only computable when actions have knowable consequences.

**What V1 is missing is not variance but consequence.** The "sweat" the designer
asked for has three deterministic sources, and V1 has none of them:

- **pending commitment** — you have acted, and must wait while it develops (Step 1);
- **irreversibility under incomplete information** — you must run before you know
  (Step 4);
- **a deadline with a visible price of delay** (Step 2).

That is the whole program in one line.

*(If ever revisited: the rule is chance you **buy**, never chance you're dealt — an
opt-in SALVO order is defensible, a percentage intercept is not, and any roll must
derive from a public hash of (round, origin hex) so replays stay exact for the V1.5
server and every loss stays explainable.)*

## 5. The eight steps

Ordered by impact per unit of work; each is one session, independently shippable and
independently soak-measurable. **Steps 1 and 2 were added on 2026-08-30**, after the
first draft, at the designer's request; they moved to the front because both are
smaller than flight time and both attack numbers this document already indicts.

### Step 1 — Battle reports: tell the player what just happened

*Added 2026-08-30 at the designer's request: "players need to be alerted with large
notifications if their strikes are successful or if they are struck themselves."*

Pure UI over the already-filtered log — no rule changes, no balance risk — and it
builds **first** because it is the reveal layer every later step speaks through: Step
3's inbound-strike warning and Step 5's convoy alarm are both battle reports before
they are anything else.

**What fires, and the reason the list is short.** A confirmed-kill banner **only when
a launcher is destroyed**; the four outcome banners at match end (victory or defeat by
decapitation, victory or defeat by disarmament, plus the draws and capitulation). That
is the whole set, and the restraint is a rules decision rather than a scoping one:

- **A bunker hit shows nothing, to either player.** `BUNKER_HIT` is owner-only and
  stays log-only. Telling the *attacker* a hit landed would turn blind fire into a
  bunker detector and delete the silence-vs-destruction tell that identifies the real
  bunker (§6, §12) — the game's signature mechanic, traded for a popup.
- **A destroyed decoy or interceptor base shows nothing.** Both are public
  `UNIT_DESTROYED` events and both stay in the log; the agreed rule is "confirmed kill
  means a launcher."
- **An impact on empty ground shows nothing** — a banner there would make the
  *absence* of a banner informative, which is the exact leak `IMPACT` exists to avoid.

**The hotseat rule that makes it safe.** Banners are per-seat and must never render
while a handoff is pending — a full-screen "LAUNCHER LOST" appearing as the wrong
player sits down is the same class of leak gotchas 36/57 govern. Structurally: the
report layer reads `viewer` internally (never takes a `PlayerId` from a caller), and
`App` mounts it only on the board screen, which it *swaps* away from during a handoff
rather than overlaying (gotcha 58).

**Two calls deliberately left open** for playtest, both one-line changes and neither
built by default: an amber, defender-only alarm when *your own* bunker is struck
(leaks nothing — the event is already in that player's log and never reaches the
attacker — and it is the most direct reading of "alerted if they are struck
themselves"); and a banner when a decoy dies, which is the biggest gut-punch in the
game for both sides at once.

### Step 2 — One interceptor base, and its location is public

*Added 2026-08-30 at the designer's request: two hidden bases "bring the gameplay down
a lot."* Correct, and the harness had already said so from the other direction — 0.01
bases destroyed and 0.3 intercepts per side per match. The system was invisible,
unkillable, and irrelevant.

`placementCounts.interceptor` drops 2 → 1, and each base is filed as a permanent
static reveal in the *opponent's* intel at `startMatch`. Implementation cost is
near-zero and needs no new types: a public base is simply a static asset that is
already spotted on turn one, and the permanent-reveal machinery plus `forgetDestroyed`
already handle the rest.

**Why this is a better game, not merely a cleaner one:**

- **Every drone death becomes the player's own navigational error.** Drones die 2.3
  times a match to bubbles nobody can see, costing ~4.6 blind rounds out of 13. That
  is the "brings gameplay down" feeling exactly — a deterministic game punishing you
  with information you were never allowed to have.
- **It attacks §1's central finding.** The ≥3 exclusion means a public base publishes
  a 19-hex disc in which no bunker or decoy can be: the 96-hex search space becomes
  ~77 for free, and the drone can now sweep *around* the one known bubble instead of
  into it. Both of the numbers strangling the game — 26% coverage, 48% of sides ever
  finding a site — are downstream of this.
- **One base is a commitment where two were a hedge.** A single public base forces the
  defender to choose one lane to protect, and that choice is a *statement* the enemy
  reads. The play it opens is the good one: **site the base to cover the approach to
  your decoy**, so the attacker reasons "they would only defend the real one" and
  spends missiles on the fake. That is a deliberate lie available at setup — the
  defender's first bluff, arriving four steps before the convoy exists.
- **It deletes spec complexity.** §11's "bases are inferred, never photographed"
  section and gotcha 20 both become moot, and `DRONE_DOWNED` / `MISSILE_INTERCEPTED`
  stop being 7-candidate riddles.

**The companion number to weigh, not to assume.** One base at radius 1 covers 7 hexes
where two covered 14 — a halving of defensive weight on top of making it avoidable.
Raising `interceptorCoverageRadius` to 2 (19 hexes, one contiguous readable zone)
restores the weight while keeping the single commitment, and is newly available
because gotcha 20's constraint existed only to stop drones photographing bases. If it
goes to 2, `bunkerExclusionRadius` likely follows 3 → 4 to preserve "no point-blank
shield." **Soak the count change alone first**, so the two effects are separable.

**The risk to watch is the opposite of the current failure.** If the hunt becomes so
easy that matches end before the midgame has any tension, the levers are
`droneRespawnDelay` and the radii above — never re-hiding the base.


### Step 3 — Missile flight time *(the keystone of the rules program)*

**Missiles advance `RULES.missileSpeed` (first draft: 4) hexes per resolution.** A
shot at range ≤ 4 lands the same round, exactly as today; a shot at range 5–6
spends one round visibly in flight and lands in the *next* round's impact phase.

Nearly free, because the information is already public: `LAUNCH_DETECTED` already
tells both players `{origin, target}` (§6) — today it is simply published too late
to act on. Moving impact one round downstream converts a resolved event into a
**standing threat**, with zero new visibility rules. What it buys:

- **The defender's first reaction window** — the first defensive agency in the game
  (cause 3). A long-range shot names its target hex a full order phase before it
  lands.
- **Firing publishes what you believe.** A blind probe now tells the enemy where
  you are searching a round before you learn anything. Firing becomes a confession.
- **Range becomes tempo, not just reach.** Close-in (≤ 4) counter-battery keeps
  today's certainty; long-range fire is telegraphed and dodgeable. To deny the
  enemy their warning you must close to 4 — deep into their reach. Every firing
  position is now a real decision.
- **Suppression, emergent and deterministic.** A launcher expecting inbound fire
  must move, and moving means not firing. Long-range fire now suppresses even when
  it misses — an artillery truth the game gets from phase order alone.
- **The 2-missile alpha strike is telegraphed** from range 5–6, which is what makes
  Steps 3 and 4 (the defender's answers) meaningful rather than decorative.

Sim change is small: `flyMissiles` (`src/sim/missiles.ts`) already advances every
missile one hex at a time with per-step intercept checks; the change is to persist
unfinished flights in `GameState` and advance them `missileSpeed` steps per round.
Full brief with types-for-approval and the five spec rulings it needs (in-flight
missiles vs disarmament, dead-hand interaction, tiebreaks, phase-4 freeze, the
visibility shape): **`docs/next-session-flight-time.md`**. Spec: §3, §4, §6 (note),
§7, §10.

### Step 4 — DEFCON: the round cap becomes a threat, not a refuge

A third of hard matches end in Armistice — the design admitting nobody was ever
forced to act. Replace the flat draw with an escalation ladder:

- Match opens at **DEFCON 5**; drops one step every 5 rounds — and one step on
  every bunker-or-decoy hit, so aggression accelerates the clock and escalation is
  itself a purchasable tempo weapon.
- **DEFCON 3:** intercepts become 1 per *player* per round, not per base — defense
  degrades.
- **DEFCON 2:** missile range +2 for both sides — the board shrinks.
- **DEFCON 1:** the round cap resolves as **Mutual Annihilation — both lose** —
  not a draw. Turtling is no longer a result.

Cheapest change on the list: a few `defs.ts` numbers, one branch in `adjudicate`
(`src/sim/outcomes.ts`), a public counter in state. Gives the game the arc cause 5
says it lacks. Spec: §4 row 6, §7.

### ~~Interception becomes a decision: reveal to defend~~ — SUPERSEDED by Step 2

*Was Step 3 in the first draft: an intercept would be optional and would publish the
intercepting base's hex.* **Struck on 2026-08-30**, because a base that is public from
setup has nothing left to reveal. Its value did not disappear — it moved earlier and
got cheaper: the ≥3 exclusion carve now happens at setup (Step 2), and the bait
dynamic it wanted emerges for free once Step 3 lands, since one intercept per round
against missiles arriving across rounds already makes a cheap probe a real play.

**Held in reserve, if defender agency still feels thin in playtest:** make the
intercept opt-in with a one-round recharge. Declining then becomes a genuine "spend my
only shield now, or save it for what might be the real strike?" — deterministic,
needing no hidden information, and it works precisely *because* the base is public.


### Step 5 — Leader relocation: the convoy *(the designer's idea, weaponised)*

The escape idea is right, and the reason it is right is not survivability — it is
that it hands the defender **a bluff, the first since setup** (cause 4).

- New **CONVOY** order, available to the bunker **and the decoy** — §12's
  indistinguishability principle makes this mandatory, and it is also the entire
  point.
- A convoy is **loud**: it emits a public `CONVOY_DEPARTED { origin }` — origin
  only, never destination, exactly as `MARCH_DETECTED` and for the same reason
  (gotcha 51b). It becomes the third loud action (§11 rule 2 generalises again, as
  it did for the forced march).
- Range ~3, resolves in **phase 5** — so a missile already in the air lands first.
  Combined with Step 1: when the inbound-strike warning sounds, **you must start
  running before you know it was aimed at you.** That is the sweat, deterministic.
- The site's permanent static reveal is cleared on departure — the enemy knows it
  left, not where it went. Their map is now *wrong by their own hand*: they made
  you move.

The reasoning ladder it creates: a convoy means *someone here is frightened* —
evidence, but of what? Run the real bunker when found and it survives but confirms
it was real. Run the **decoy** when found and the enemy believes the real bunker
escaped their trap — and chases a ghost. Convoy the decoy in response to blind fire
that landed nowhere near anything, and you convince them their search is working,
steering the whole hunt into the wrong quadrant. Every option is a statement about
what you want them to believe. Fully deterministic. Spec: §2, §3, §6 (new event),
§11, §12.

*(Balance guard: a convoy per site per match, or a cooldown — unlimited relocation
would delete the "static sightings are permanent" pillar rather than complicate it.
First draft: each site may convoy once.)*

### Step 6 — The deception toolkit

Cause 4's direct answer, two cheap moves:

1. **Decoy emissions** (promote from `v2-backlog.md`, where it already lives): a
   decoy may emit one fake `LAUNCH_DETECTED` per match. This poisons the game's
   single most trustworthy channel — a launch contact stops being *proof* of a
   launcher, and counter-battery becomes a read instead of a reflex. One fake per
   match keeps the channel mostly honest, which is what keeps the fake valuable.
2. **Name the bait** (UI/teaching only, zero rules): firing from inside your own
   interceptor coverage to invite an interceptable counter-battery shot is already
   legal, already named in §7, and never played. Steps 1 and 3 make it strong;
   teach it in the order panel rather than building anything.

### Step 7 — The dead hand becomes fearsome

Today the game's most dramatic moment is a shrug: the decapitated player fires at a
bunker they usually never found, into working interceptors. Change: **the dead-hand
volley ignores interception** (a launch-on-warning salvo saturates — thematically
exact, mechanically decisive). The knock-on is the prize: the attacker now wants to
**kill launchers first**, which turns Disarmament from a rarely-fired alternate win
(5% of outcomes) into the *prerequisite for a safe decapitation* — a whole
strategic layer, from one deleted check. Optional companion: a secretly
pre-designated standing retaliation hex, so the volley is a decision made earlier
under uncertainty rather than an afterthought. Spec: §3, §4, §10.

### Step 8 — Asymmetry: doctrines, not classes *(build last)*

Recommend **against** new unit types — §2's "depth from interactions, not roster
size" discipline is correct and worth defending. The deliverable form of asymmetry:
**a doctrine is one secret card chosen at setup that re-tunes 2–3 existing numbers
and adds at most one order.** No new units, no new art — a modifier table over
`RULES`/`UNIT_DEFS`, inheriting every existing rule for free.

The load-bearing property: **your doctrine is hidden, and the opponent infers it
from your play.** That is "put yourself in the enemy's position" delivered
directly — by round 6 a good player has read your doctrine and is counter-playing
it; a great one has been feeding you a false read of theirs.

| Doctrine | Gets | Pays | The tell | The counter |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **First Strike** | launcher move 4; flight time −1 round | missile range 5; only 1 base | forward early, shoots close | stay outside 5, kill the lone base, play long |
| **Granite** | bunker 3 hits; exclusion radius 2 | launcher move 2; no MARCH | slow, cedes the middle | disarm them — they cannot maneuver |
| **Maskirovka** | 2 decoys; each fakes one launch | only 2 launchers | three sites, thin fire | don't test sites — disarm |
| **Air Defence** | 3 bases; 2 intercepts each | drone respawn 4; range 5 | intercepts freely, shallow recon | saturate one base; out-recon |
| **Deep Recon** | drone range 8, respawn 1, swath radius 2 | exclusion radius 5; 1 base | wide fast sweeps by round 2 | every base found carves a radius-5 hole around their sites |
| **Dead Hand** | retaliation doubled, unstoppable, triggers on disarmament too | coverage radius 0 (own hex) | invisible early — that's the point | disarm before you decapitate |

Note Deep Recon: its strength is paid for with a rule that makes its own sites
*easier to deduce*. That reciprocity is the design bar for every doctrine — each
must (a) change how you look on the board, (b) have a counter a reader can play,
(c) touch only numbers already in `defs.ts`. Build last: it is a balance multiplier
over everything above it, meaningless to tune before the core loop poses questions.

## 6. How we will know it worked

Baseline is §1's table (seed 3, 60/pairing). Every step re-runs it before/after
(CLAUDE.md already mandates this for `defs.ts`/`cpu.ts` changes). Acceptance —
and the head-to-head is the one that matters, because it *is* the complaint:

| Metric | Now | Target |
|---|---|---|
| **hard vs medium** | **38–30, 52 draws** | **≥ 70–20, ≤ 30 draws** |
| Armistice, hard mirror | 33% | < 10% |
| Missiles fired /side/match | 3.6 | > 8 |
| Missiles intercepted /side | 0.3 | > 1.5 |
| Bases destroyed /side | 0.01 | > 0.3 |
| Sides that ever find a site | 48% | > 80% |

**Two metrics `scripts/soak.ts` must gain**, because they measure the actual goal:

- **Belief error** — mean false entries on each player's map per round. Today it is
  structurally **zero** (information is monotonic and always true); after Steps 4–5
  it becomes the headline number. A strategy game's health can be read off how
  often players are *confidently wrong*.
- **Contested rounds** — share of rounds where a side held both a legal LAUNCH at a
  known target *and* a meaningful MOVE. The cheapest proxy for "did a decision
  exist this round?"

## 7. Sequencing and session protocol

One step per session, spec amended in the same change, before/after soak attached to
the session's status entry — the established workflow.

1. **Battle reports** (§5 Step 1). UI only, and it must **measure inert**: soak output
   byte-identical apart from timestamp lines, the existing suite green unchanged.
2. **Single public base** (§5 Step 2). The first real balance movement of the program;
   soak the count change alone before touching either radius.
3. **Missile flight time** (§5 Step 3), per `docs/next-session-flight-time.md`. Its
   named follow-ups — CPU dodge-on-warning and target-leading, UI drawing flights from
   the log — are their own sessions, and the CPU one is where the head-to-head
   acceptance target gets measured for real.

Steps 4–8 then land in plan order, each re-gated on its own soak. Two items that
predate this consultation fold into those follow-ups rather than disappearing: CPU and
balance tuning, and animation from the event log. The V1.5 WebSocket server (build
order step 11+) waits behind the program.

**One CPU note carried forward from Step 2:** once the enemy base is public, the CPU's
drone should route around it rather than through it — `sweepLanes` currently flies a
serpentine that ignores danger entirely. Until that follow-up lands, the CPU will keep
feeding its drone to a bubble it can now see, so read the first post-Step-2 soak with
that in mind: the human-facing improvement is larger than the numbers will show.
